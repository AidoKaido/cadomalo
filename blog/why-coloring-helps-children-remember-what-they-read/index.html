Why coloring helps children remember what they read

Playful learning 5 min read 28 August 2026

We designed every Cadomalo book so that children read a page, then colour it. It is not decoration, and it is not there just to fill a rainy afternoon. For us, the colouring pages are the part of the book that turns a story into something your child owns.

Colouring is one of the oldest, simplest activities of early childhood. It needs no batteries, no password and no screen. In this article we explain why we think it pairs so well with reading, how to make the most of it, and how to keep it relaxed rather than another task on the list.

Slowing down helps

Colouring a picture of the lungs takes ten minutes. That is ten minutes of looking closely at shapes, colours and details a child would have skipped past on a screen or while flipping pages.

When a story moves quickly, young children catch the main idea and move on. Colouring invites them to stay. They notice that the lungs come in a pair, that the heart sits between them, that the ribs curve around like a cage. Nobody had to point it out — they found it themselves, crayon in hand.

Looking is learning

Before a child can colour a shape, they have to see it properly: where it starts, where it ends, what sits next to it. That careful looking is a skill in itself, and it is the same skill they will use later to read diagrams, maps and charts.

Hands help heads

When children do something with what they have just heard, they tend to remember it better. Choosing a colour for a muscle is a tiny decision about what that muscle is. Is it red like the meat in a sandwich? Pink like their cheeks? Bright purple because that is their favourite colour? Each choice is a small moment of thinking about the subject.

This is one reason we prefer “read, then colour” to “colour while you listen”. The story gives the idea; the colouring gives the child time to do something with it.

It is good practice for little hands too

Holding a crayon, staying (more or less) inside the lines and pressing harder or softer are all part of developing the fine motor control that writing will need later. There is no need to turn this into a lesson. Simply having crayons and paper within reach is enough.

It opens conversations

Colouring side by side is one of the easiest times to talk. Nobody has to make eye contact, nobody is in a hurry, and the picture gives you something to talk about. “Why did you make the stomach purple?” can lead to a surprisingly good chat about food and energy.

Some conversation starters that work well while colouring a body page:

  • “What does this part of the body do?”
  • “Where is it on you? Can you point to it?”
  • “What would happen if we didn’t have it?”
  • “What colour do you think it really is?”
  • “What do you want to find out next?”

If your child answers with something that isn’t quite right, there is no need to correct them directly. Repeat the right idea in your own sentence and keep colouring. For more on handling these questions, see how to talk to young children about their body.

Keep it relaxed

Colouring helps most when it feels like play. A few things that keep it that way:

  • No “inside the lines” rule. Scribbling is a perfectly good stage.
  • Any colour is allowed. A green heart is fine — talk about the real colour if they ask.
  • Short is fine. Five minutes of colouring is still five minutes of looking.
  • Let them stop. Unfinished pages can wait for another day.
  • Praise the effort, not the result. “You worked so carefully on that bone” beats “that’s beautiful”.

Younger children may enjoy one big shape with thick crayons; older children may want fine pencils and small details. Both are learning.

Try this tonight

Here is a simple routine that takes about fifteen minutes:

  1. Read one double page of a story together.
  2. Ask one question about it — just one.
  3. Let your child colour the matching page while you chat.
  4. Ask them to tell you, or a teddy, what the page is about.
  5. Keep the finished page in a folder.

Over a few weeks, the folder becomes your child’s own science book. They will love flipping back through it, and every page reminds them of what they learned. That moment of retelling is especially valuable: explaining something to someone else is a great way for a child to check what they understood.

In short

Colouring is not just a way to keep children busy. Paired with a story, it slows them down, gives their hands something to do with what they just heard, and opens easy conversations. Keep it relaxed, keep the pages, and let your child retell what they coloured. Small, calm moments like these are often the ones that stick.
