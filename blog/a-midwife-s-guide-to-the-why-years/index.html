A midwife’s guide to the “why?” years

From our midwife 6 min read 14 August 2026

Somewhere around the age of three, many children discover the most powerful word in the world: why. Why is the sky blue? Why do I have to sleep? Why does my tummy rumble? Why, why, why? It can be wonderful and exhausting in the same afternoon.

If you are in the middle of this phase, take heart. The questions are a sign that something important is happening, and there are simple ways to welcome them without feeling you must deliver an encyclopaedia on demand. Here is how we think about the “why?” years, and what tends to help.

It’s a good sign

“Why” means your child has noticed that things have causes. Rain comes from clouds, a tower falls when you pull the bottom block, a plant droops when it is thirsty. Spotting those links is the beginning of scientific thinking — and it deserves to be encouraged.

Not every “why” is a request for facts, though. Sometimes it means “I want to keep talking to you”. Sometimes it means “I don’t want to do that”, as in “why do I have to put my shoes on?”. And sometimes it is a game: the fun of seeing how long the chain can go. Learning to tell these apart makes the whole phase much easier.

Three kinds of “why”

  • The curious why: “Why do cats have whiskers?” Your child genuinely wants to know.
  • The connection why: a string of questions at bedtime. Your child wants your attention and closeness.
  • The resistance why: “Why do I have to brush my teeth?” Your child is testing a rule.

Each one deserves a slightly different response. The curious why gets an answer or an investigation. The connection why gets warmth, even if the answer is short. The resistance why gets a calm, brief reason and a kind but firm limit.

Keep answers short and true

Young children take in one idea at a time. A short, honest answer is better than a long, complete one. “Your tummy rumbles because it is squeezing and moving food and air along” is plenty for a four-year-old. If they want more, they will ask another why — and now you know what they are curious about.

Try to avoid answers that are simple but wrong. “Because I said so” and made-up explanations are tempting on a tired day, but children often remember them. It is better to say “That’s a big question — let’s look it up tomorrow” than to invent something you will have to undo later.

Turn the question around

“What do you think?” is a perfectly good answer. Children often have a theory already, and sharing it builds confidence. You may be surprised by how thoughtful their ideas are — or delighted by how creative.

When they share a theory, treat it like a scientist would. “Interesting! How could we check?” turns a guess into a little experiment. Does the ice melt faster in the sun or in the shade? Does a feather fall as fast as a spoon? Testing their own idea teaches far more than being told the answer.

“Let’s find out” is magic

You don’t need to know everything. Looking it up together in a book shows that not knowing is the start of learning, not a failure. It also teaches a valuable habit: when you have a question, there are ways to find the answer.

Good places to look together:

  • Picture books and simple non-fiction — ideal for questions about animals, nature and the body.
  • The world around you — a walk, a garden, a kitchen experiment.
  • People who know — a grandparent, a librarian, a doctor or dentist at the next check-up.

Keep a question jar

On busy days, write the question on a scrap of paper and put it in a jar. At the weekend, pick one and explore it properly. Nothing gets lost, and nobody feels brushed off.

The jar also helps on days when your patience is running low. “That is such a good question that I want to give it proper time. Let’s put it in the jar” is honest and kind. Your child learns that their questions matter, even when the answer has to wait.

Make the weekend exploration special

  1. Let your child pick a question from the jar.
  2. Decide together how to explore it: a book, an experiment, a drawing or a walk.
  3. Spend fifteen or twenty minutes on it — no more is needed.
  4. Draw or colour what you found, and keep it in a folder.

Look after yourself too

Answering questions all day is tiring, and it is perfectly fine to need a break. “I’ve answered lots of questions, and now my brain needs a rest. Let’s ask the next one after dinner” is a reasonable boundary. Children cope well with limits when they are given calmly and kept consistently.

Every child develops at their own pace. If anything about your child’s speech, understanding or behaviour worries you, talk to your child’s doctor.

In short

The “why?” years are a sign of a curious, growing mind. Keep answers short and true, turn questions back to your child, explore together when you don’t know, and use a question jar on busy days. Above all, let your child see that questions are welcome. That feeling will stay with them long after the questions change.
