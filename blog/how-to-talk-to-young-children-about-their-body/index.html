How to talk to young children about their body

Human body 6 min read 18 September 2026

Between the ages of three and eight, children discover that their body is not just something that runs and jumps — it is something that works. Suddenly they want to know why their heart beats faster after a race, where their breakfast went, or why a scraped knee grows a crusty scab before it heals.

These questions are a gift. They show that your child is paying attention to the world closest to them: their own body. And they give you a chance to share something precious — the idea that their body is clever, capable and worth looking after. You don’t need a science degree to answer well. You need a few simple habits, honest words and a little patience. Here is the approach we use in every Cadomalo book.

Use the real words

Lungs, stomach, skeleton, brain. Children handle real words beautifully when they come with a picture and a feeling. “Your lungs are like two balloons that fill up when you breathe in” is simple and true. There is no need to invent cute substitutes: a four-year-old who can say “dinosaur” can say “intestine”.

Real words also travel well. When your child hears “stomach” at the doctor’s, at school or in a book, they will recognise it straight away. That recognition is a small but real boost of confidence. If you speak more than one language at home, the same applies in each of them — we share some ideas in our gentle habits for multilingual families.

Pair each word with an image

A word on its own is easy to forget. A word attached to a picture sticks. Try comparisons from your child’s everyday life:

  • The heart is a pump about the size of their fist.
  • The stomach is a stretchy bag that mixes food like a washing machine.
  • Bones are the strong frame of a house; muscles are the ropes that pull it.
  • The skin is a waterproof suit that keeps germs out.

Comparisons are never perfect, and that is fine. As long as the main idea is true, your child will refine the details as they grow.

Start with what they can feel

Put their hand on their chest while they breathe. Let them feel their tummy after a snack. Ask them to squeeze a fist and watch the muscle in their arm. The body is the best science lab they will ever own, and it is always with them.

A few easy “experiments” you can try at home, with no equipment at all:

  • Heartbeat: feel it at rest, then again after twenty jumps. What changed?
  • Breathing: lie down with a soft toy on their tummy and watch it rise and fall.
  • Bones: gently feel the knobbly bones in the wrist, knee and spine.
  • Senses: close your eyes and guess objects by touch, or sounds by ear.

Each little discovery turns an abstract fact into something your child has noticed for themselves. That is the kind of knowledge that lasts. If you’d like more ideas like these, we have gathered a whole list in 15 human body activities for preschoolers.

Answer the question they asked — not more

A four-year-old asking “why do I have bones?” wants one good answer, not a lecture. “So you can stand up tall — without bones you’d be wobbly like jelly!” is perfect. If they want more, they will ask.

It is tempting to share everything we know, especially when a child seems interested. But young children take in one idea at a time. A short answer leaves room for their next question, and their next question tells you exactly where their curiosity is heading. Follow it.

When you don’t know the answer

You will not know everything, and that is perfectly fine. “I’m not sure — let’s find out together” is one of the best things a child can hear. It shows that grown-ups keep learning too, and it turns a question into a shared project. We talk more about this in our guide to the “why?” years.

Keep it gentle and positive

Children absorb the tone of our answers as much as the words. Talk about the body with curiosity and respect: it is amazing, it works hard, and it deserves care. Avoid making any part of it sound disgusting or shameful. Even burps, sneezes and poo can be explained calmly — they are simply the body doing its job.

This positive tone matters for health habits too. A child who understands that teeth help them chew and smile is more likely to see brushing as looking after a friend rather than a chore. A child who knows that sleep helps their body grow may find bedtime a little easier to accept.

When a question touches on pain, illness or something that worries you, keep the answer simple and reassuring, and check with your child’s doctor if you have any health concerns.

Turn it into a ritual

Pick one body part a week. Read about it, colour it, look for it in the bath or on a walk. Small, repeated moments build knowledge that lasts much longer than a single big explanation.

A weekly theme can be as light as you like:

  1. Monday: read a short story about this week’s body part.
  2. Midweek: try one feel-it-yourself experiment.
  3. Weekend: colour a page or draw it from memory, then tell someone else what they learned.

Telling another person — a grandparent, a sibling, a teddy bear — is a lovely way for children to check what they understood. If they get something slightly wrong, gently repeat the right idea without making it a correction.

In short

Talking to young children about their body does not require expertise. It requires real words, simple images, hands-on moments and short, honest answers. Follow your child’s questions, keep the tone warm, and come back to the topic often. Little by little, they will build a picture of their body as something wonderful — and something worth looking after.
