15 human body activities for preschoolers

Human body 7 min read 10 November 2026

Preschoolers are natural scientists, and the most fascinating laboratory they own is their own body. It moves, it breathes, it makes noises, it grows. The best part: you do not need special equipment to explore it. Everything below uses things you probably already have in a kitchen drawer or a toy box.

We have grouped our 15 favourite human body activities by theme, so you can pick one for a rainy afternoon or build a whole “body week”. Each one takes between five and twenty minutes.

Always supervise young children during these activities. Some use small objects, string or food: keep small items away from children under 3, and adapt anything that involves food to your child’s allergies and needs.

Our five senses

1. The mystery sound game

Hide a few everyday sounds behind your back: keys jingling, paper crumpling, a spoon tapping a cup, water pouring. Ask your child to close their eyes and guess. Then swap roles. It is a lovely way to talk about ears and how they catch sounds all around us.

2. The texture detective bag

Put a few objects with different textures in a cloth bag or an old sock: a sponge, a smooth stone, a piece of fabric, a pine cone. Your child reaches in without looking and describes what they feel: soft, rough, bumpy, cold. Our skin tells us so much without our eyes.

3. Smell and guess

Prepare three or four small cups with familiar smells: an orange peel, a little cinnamon, a slice of banana, some coffee grounds. With eyes closed, can your child recognise them? Talk about how the nose helps us enjoy food and notice danger, like smoke.

Heart and lungs

4. Feel the heartbeat

Ask your child to place their hand flat on the left side of their chest and keep very still. Can they feel a gentle thump? Then jump twenty times together and feel it again. Faster! The heart works harder when we move, to send more blood around the body.

5. The homemade stethoscope

Roll a sheet of card into a tube, or use the cardboard tube from a kitchen roll. Put one end against your chest and let your child listen at the other end, gently, in a quiet room. Then swap. If you have a funnel and a short piece of hose, it works even better.

6. Balloon breathing

Breathing in and out slowly is a great calming exercise as well as a science lesson. Ask your child to put both hands on their tummy and breathe in, slowly, as if filling a balloon, then breathe out like a deflating balloon. Explain that the air goes into the lungs, which fill up and empty again, like two soft sponges.

7. The straw race

Give each player a straw and a small ball of paper or a cotton ball. Who can blow it across the table to the finish line? It shows that the air from our lungs is strong enough to move things. Keep straws for children old enough to use them safely.

Bones and muscles

8. The shadow skeleton

In a dim room, shine a torch on the wall and make shadows with your hands. Can your child feel the bones in their fingers? Count the knuckles together. Talk about how bones give our body its shape, and how without them we would be wobbly like jelly.

9. The bend test

Ask your child to try picking up a toy without bending their elbows, then without bending their knees. It is surprisingly hard and very funny. This is how we discover joints: the places where bones meet so we can bend and move.

10. The muscle squeeze

Ask your child to make a fist and squeeze, while their other hand rests on their forearm. They will feel the muscle tighten, then relax when they let go. Muscles work by pulling, and they get stronger when we use them. Try it with the leg, too, by standing on tiptoe.

11. The body outline

Lay your child down on a large sheet of paper, or a few sheets taped together, and trace around them. Then draw in what you know is inside: a heart, two lungs, a stomach, a brain, some bones. It does not need to be accurate; it is a conversation starter that can stay on the wall for weeks.

Food and growing

12. Digestion in a bag

Put a few crackers in a zip-top bag, add a little water and let your child squish it with their hands. This is a little like what the stomach does: it squeezes and mixes our food into a mush. Seal the bag well and keep it as a squishy experiment, not a snack.

13. The chewing challenge

At snack time, ask your child to notice what their teeth do. The front teeth bite, the back teeth grind. Try chewing a piece of apple slowly and talk about how chewing is the first step of digestion. It is also a chance to remind them why we brush our teeth.

14. The growing chart

Mark your child’s height on a strip of paper on the wall and add the date. Measure their hand and foot too, and trace them. Come back in a few months and compare. Children love seeing proof that their body is growing, even when they cannot feel it.

15. The body museum

At the end of your body week, invite your child to set up a “museum” of everything they made: the body outline, the drawings, the growing chart, the squished cracker bag. They can be the guide and explain each item to a grandparent or a teddy. Explaining something is one of the best ways to remember it.

Turning activities into a learning journey

These 15 activities work well on their own, but children often learn most when activities connect to each other. Pick one theme a week, read about it, try an experiment, then draw or colour what you discovered. We explain why colouring helps in why colouring helps children remember what they read.

Keep your explanations short and true. If your child asks a question you cannot answer, “Let’s find out together” is a perfect answer; our guide to the “why?” years has more ideas.

In short

Exploring the human body with preschoolers does not take a lab coat, only curiosity and a few household objects. Listen to a heartbeat, trace a body outline, squish a cracker bag. Each small experiment gives your child new words, new questions and the wonderful feeling that their own body is worth discovering.
